I’m Rene Gomez. I bring mentoring, wellness coaching, and years of walking alongside people into conversations about how we live and what matters to us.

Meet Rene Gomez

Understanding you
comes first.

My work brings together several influences: mentoring, coaching, pastoral care, and counseling experience. Mentoring and coaching best describe how I like to work with people—with room for honest conversation, reflection, and practical change.

More than 15 years of pastoral care and counseling experience have taught me to listen carefully, be patient, and respect the story each person brings. That experience continues to shape my work. Before trying to solve a problem, I want to understand the person living with it.

I don’t see you as a project to be fixed. Change can be difficult, uneven, and deeply personal. I want our conversations to hold both compassion for what makes change hard and personal responsibility for the steps you choose to take.

How I work

Space to reflect.
Something to put into practice.

I prefer to shape our work together around your needs, your season of life, and what surfaces as we talk. We can follow a question that needs more attention while keeping sight of what you want to work toward. The conversation has room to move, and we decide together where to focus.

My coaching background contributes practical tools for goals, habits, wellness, and behavior change. We can use those tools to choose realistic actions, build accountability, and reflect on what you’re learning as you follow through.

Mentoring makes room for perspective and the deeper questions: who you are, what you value, how you relate to others, and where you’re headed. Some conversations lead to a clear next step. Others need time and reflection before that step becomes clear.

I trained at Mayo Clinic and am a National Board Certified Health & Wellness Coach (NBC-HWC). My Master of Divinity (MDiv) and pastoral care experience also inform how I listen and think alongside you. Together, these influences help me bring practical tools and a broader view of your life to our work.

Change and acceptance

How I Think About Change

I don’t believe needing to change means there is something fundamentally wrong with you. Accepting yourself can include being honest about what needs attention. I see self-acceptance and change as things that can support each other.

I value honest self-examination, especially when the truth is uncomfortable. That means naming a choice, a habit, or harm done clearly enough to take responsibility for it. I want to make room for conviction without condemnation: “I need to change how I handled that” gives us something to work with; “I’m a terrible person” can make honesty harder.

I think virtues help give change direction: courage, humility, wisdom, empathy, responsibility, discipline, and acceptance. In daily life, that might mean having a difficult conversation, admitting a mistake, considering someone else’s needs, or following through on a commitment.

I don’t have a formula for how growth should unfold. I often find small experiments more useful than trying to reinvent your whole life: try something manageable, reflect on what happened, and adjust. A step can be worth taking even when it doesn’t settle everything.

I don’t expect change to make life free of pain. Some pain is unavoidable, but avoidance, denial, resentment, impulsiveness, or neglect can add suffering. Alongside “What is happening to me?” I find it useful to ask, gently, “What am I doing that may be making this harder?” The point is to recognize what is mine to address without blaming myself for everything, then choose the next step with care.
